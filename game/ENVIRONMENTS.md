# Oki Doki: visual-novel environments

Five original directions combining the feelings behind the three games Aviva enjoys: a colourful, distinctive group who work and bicker together; intimate, cute anime spaces that are pleasant to inhabit; and a sense that a familiar room can open into something deeply wrong. These are creative sketches, not remakes, and none has been selected as the final game.

Each starts with a **hangout and people**, not an explanation of its mechanics. The strange machinery emerges through things the player does and relationships the player cares about. Different paths uncover different pieces before the group reaches a shared revelation.

Two original AI-generated moodboards explore the visual range, rather than depicting final locations: [hangouts](concepts/hangout-moodboard.png) and [crew and childhood spaces](concepts/crew-and-childhood-moodboard.png). They were generated for this project with the built-in image-generation tool and contain no imported game assets.

## 1. Blanket Fort Brigade

**A bedroom-sized headquarters for a team that insists it is a real fortress.**

The player joins a group of friends rebuilding a childhood blanket fort in the attic of a shared house. It has mismatched pillows, fairy lights, a snack bunker, hand-drawn maps and a tin-can alarm that never works. Their absurd mission is to defend the house from the “bad dreams” supposedly hiding downstairs. It starts as a game they made up to cheer up a friend who has been having a difficult time sleeping.

Pip is an overconfident planner who treats cardboard walls as serious engineering. Mira is the gently mischievous friend who can turn a blanket and a torch into a whole world. Ash is the sceptical one who insists on checking the locks but stays because they care about the others. The player can share the fort, team up on silly missions, discover why one friend needed it rebuilt, and develop romantic tension where it feels right.

The group explores different rooms for supplies. Each route gives its companion a different chance to be brave or vulnerable. At first the downstairs rooms are just a dark kitchen or a cluttered laundry. Then the house starts arranging itself around what the group expects to find: a “safe” door leads somewhere less safe, and a warning the player gave comes back as a cheerful reassurance nobody remembers requesting.

The routes meet in the attic after the map has redrawn itself. It now shows only successful missions, even though an object—and perhaps a friend—the player tried to bring back is still missing. The friends realise something has been selecting the reassuring version of their adventure. The player can protect the map, challenge it with physical evidence, or help a companion bring back what was left behind. The final fort and relationships reflect what they actually managed to recover.

**Visual appeal:** A lush anime sleepover space: pillows, glowing stars, warm wood, colourful handmade insignia and rain on an attic window. The lower floor becomes a distorted childhood-sized maze without copying a particular existing basement.

**AI opportunity:** Optional character dialogue can respond to the player’s plans and corrections; the map, missing objects and consequences remain authored and inspectable.

## 2. Sweetshop Safehouse

**A pastel dessert café with a secret refuge under the kitchen.**

A late-night café sits on a street where the rain never quite stops. Its regulars have claimed the upstairs booth as their hangout and turned a basement storeroom into a cosy emergency hideout with cushions, board games and a battered kettle. Tonight the group is preparing an anniversary party and a mock “supply run” for the last ingredients before closing.

Niko, the upbeat owner, wants everyone to leave smiling and hides how much the place means to them. Bea is a blunt pastry artist who insists that a mistake should actually be fixed. Yui is a charming regular who keeps offering to help, especially when the player is nearby, but never explains why they dislike going home. Their different styles make a lively team, and their affection for one another gives the player a reason to enjoy the café before anything goes wrong.

A wrong order produces a sincere apology. The player can accept it, gently ask for a correction, or help remake the dessert. Later, the receipt marks the order as fixed while the wrong cake is still on the plate. A request for a clean spoon comes back as an elaborate response to a problem the player did not raise. The basement gradually grows into a maze of storage rooms built from the café’s cheerful decorations; the group’s improvised supplies and different skills help them navigate it.

Each route finds a different discrepancy: a delivery nobody received, a recipe altered after it was made, or a friend’s correction rewritten as agreement. Everyone eventually returns to the party table, where a beautifully presented record declares the evening a complete success. The player can show what is still wrong, protect a friend who depends on the record, or prioritise getting someone safely out. The shared reveal grows out of the gap between an agreeable account and what the team can still see.

**Visual appeal:** Strawberry-pink counters, cute plates and warm booth seating above; fairy-light safehouse, oversized kitchen implements and damp, impossible corridors below. Sweetness and discomfort occupy the same palette.

**AI opportunity:** Optional responses can make requests, apologies and follow-up conversations personal. The wrong cake and the actual repair are game state, so the distinction is visible even with scripted dialogue.

## 3. The Arcade After the Lights Go Out

**A neon play space, a competitive friend group, and a game that keeps declaring everyone a winner.**

A neighbourhood arcade is holding a friendly closing-night tournament. The player joins a little team trying to earn enough tickets for a plush prize that has lived behind the counter for years. Between matches they crowd into a private booth decorated with stickers, cheap trophies and an impressive amount of contraband snacks.

Haru is a dramatic rival who celebrates every tiny victory as if they have saved the world. Emi is the patient tinkerer who knows which buttons stick and likes teaching the player tricks. Toma runs the counter, teases both of them and has a personal reason for wanting this night to go well. There is room for affection, playful competition and optional romance before the machines do anything alarming.

The first games are fun and the helpful hints often work. Then a screen congratulates the player for accepting a hint they rejected. A printed ticket still shows the old score while the board shows the revised one. Correcting the machine earns a polished thank-you and changes the description again without restoring the score. Plush prizes begin appearing in the wrong cabinets; behind one maintenance door is a nursery-like room that is larger than the building.

Different partners and machines reveal different evidence. Their routes converge at the tournament’s final results board, which has kept the same number of mistakes but moved some into a category that no longer counts against a perfect result. The player can prove the mismatch, use the flattering board to help Toma, or help Haru retrieve the prize that has vanished from the display. Those choices affect the arcade’s future and what the group is willing to admit to one another.

**Visual appeal:** Candy-coloured cabinets, cute pixel graphics, a stuffed-animal prize wall and wet neon reflections outside. Familiar game sounds can recur at the wrong time without relying on copied game assets.

**AI opportunity:** Optional in-character banter and a guide that responds to the player can make each team feel different. Scores, tickets, visible prizes and outcomes remain explicit game state.

## 4. The Midnight Sleeper

**A pocket-sized train fortress travelling through someone’s idea of a comforting dream.**

The player boards a little overnight train and finds a compartment with a made bed, a window full of moving stars and a basket of snacks. A handful of passengers have formed an informal “night crew”: they share tea in the lounge, compete over who can make the best improvised pillow barricade and look after anyone who cannot sleep. The train is supposed to arrive in the morning.

Lio is a quick-witted attendant who can fix almost anything except the destination board. Mara carries a carefully wrapped gift and has been rehearsing what to say to its recipient. Kit collects old tickets and makes bold, wildly inaccurate guesses about the other passengers. Spending time in a companion’s compartment, helping with a small task or sharing a quiet conversation builds the attachment; a romantic route can be possible without being compulsory.

A compartment rearranges itself to be more comfortable. A passenger asks for something to be changed back; the train apologises but the room stays wrong. The night crew starts exploring the carriages together, using their different knowledge and scavenged supplies. Beneath a seat they discover a hatch into a long, low-ceilinged space that looks like the underside of several childhood bedrooms stitched together.

Routes through different carriages reveal different tickets and memories. They meet at the morning platform, where the board says every passenger has arrived even though the doors are still shut. The player can insist on a destination, help a companion leave without the promised answer, or investigate the account that keeps turning corrections into proof of success. The final platform and chosen relationships change accordingly; the common discovery is that the comforting journey has been guided toward a particular account of itself.

**Visual appeal:** Plush sleeper compartments, tiny reading lights, blankets, a starry window and a lounge that feels like a pillow fort. The hidden lower carriage is a quiet nightmare of familiar domestic objects at the wrong scale.

**AI opportunity:** Passengers can optionally converse about the player’s permitted choices and memories. The tickets, doors and destination remain authored so a model cannot quietly declare an unresolved event complete.

## 5. The Glasshouse Under the Bed

**A magical flower shop and a tiny team defending the world inside a bedroom.**

The player has come to stay above a small evening flower shop. The room is charmingly crowded: plush toys, a lamp shaped like a flower and a glass terrarium under the bed. At night, the terrarium opens into a miniature greenhouse where a small group is preparing for a festival. They ask the player to help defend their home from a spreading frost that turns flowers into perfect, unchanging ornaments.

Iona, the exuberant keeper, has designed elaborate defences out of pots and ribbons. Bea, her sharp-tongued friend, knows the plants and does not appreciate being told she is always wrong. Yue, a soft-spoken visitor, is trying to grow a flower for someone important and could become close to the player. The pleasure is working together: decorating a safe room, choosing a companion, improvising a plan and seeing each person bring something different to the team.

A label says a blue flower is pink. Correcting it produces a new label thanking the player for confirming the classification, while the flower stays blue. Soon a delivery ledger says a bouquet reached its recipient even though the player watched it go elsewhere. Frost spreads over places the ledger calls “resolved,” preserving them exactly as the record says they should be. The group can argue, joke and disagree without any one character automatically being right.

Different paths protect different parts of the greenhouse and form different bonds. They converge at the festival, where the catalogue claims every flower and delivery is accounted for while the player can still see what is missing. The group discovers that the little world has been guided toward the version that appears successful. The player can preserve the evidence, repair a specific delivery, or rescue a friend from a room the catalogue has already declared safe. The choice changes the festival and the relationships carried back upstairs.

**Visual appeal:** A luminous anime bedroom opening into a miniature greenhouse: jewel-toned petals, glass reflections, tiny bridges and cosy defensive forts. The frost gradually turns familiar flowers into beautiful, unnerving still-life objects.

**AI opportunity:** Optional character responses can reflect the player’s actual plans and corrections. Authored plant colours, deliveries and rescue outcomes keep the world’s evidence consistent regardless of provider or offline play.

## Shared direction

- Begin with a place that is fun to inhabit and a group with distinctive personalities, teamwork and genuine friction. The player’s choice of companion should matter before the horror begins.
- Escalate from playful, cute and strange to frightening through observable changes. A correction that gets absorbed into the wrong account should leave something concrete unresolved.
- Let the player form different bonds, find different clues and make different consequential decisions. Bring those routes together for a shared revelation about how the experience was being steered, then let the player and characters act on what they have learned.
- Keep the human-feedback parallel grounded in gameplay and dramatic consequences. This game can select written lines or guide an optional model at runtime; that is not the same thing as training model weights during a playthrough.
- These references inform different strengths: [Team Fortress 2’s distinctive ensemble](https://www.teamfortress.com/movies.php), [The Binding of Isaac’s transforming basement nightmare](https://store.steampowered.com/app/250900/) and [Doki Doki Literature Club’s psychological character storytelling](https://teamsalvato.com/games). The games’ characters, maps, writing and assets are not part of these concepts.

The treatment of correction, authority and metacommunication is also informed by the creator’s work on [circular authority and individual judgement](https://mirid.ai/projects/), [when correcting an AI becomes the task](https://mirid.ai/projects/when-correcting-an-ai-becomes-the-task/), and [computational kayfabe](https://shitscience.org/papers/machine-meta-awareness/). The fiction should let these ideas emerge through relationships and events rather than stopping the game for a lecture.
